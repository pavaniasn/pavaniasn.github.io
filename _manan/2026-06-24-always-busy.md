Paul Graham has a short essay called *How to Lose Time and Money* where he makes a point about fake work — work that looks like work, feels like work, but produces nothing of consequence. Because it doesn't look like self-indulgence, the internal alarm never rings. Cal Newport, in *Deep Work*, gives that distinction a vocabulary: deep work, which is cognitively demanding and requires your specific thinking, and shallow work, which is necessary and often urgent but could be done by almost anyone with reasonable training.

I read both with a sense of recognition. But the question that stayed with me was not theirs. It was mine.

A real workday has both kinds of work. Always. There is no version of professional life where you only do one. You think, you build, you figure something out — and you also coordinate, respond, attend, manage the logistics of being in an organisation. Both are real. Both are necessary. The proportion, though, is not fixed.

What I've been noticing — and I mean noticing over years, not just days — is that the combination shifts as I move through different roles and phases of my career. Early on, a large part of the work is responsive: understanding context, learning how things function, doing what's asked well. The shallow end, by Newport's definition, dominates — not because you're less capable, but because that's the nature of where you're standing. As you move up, or across, or deeper into an area of work, something changes. The expectation shifts. The work that's actually yours — the judgment calls, the things only you can build or say or decide — starts to grow.

But here's what I find myself sitting with: how much of that shift happens to me, and how much do I have a hand in it?

Because I think it can go either way. It's entirely possible to be in a senior role and still spend most of your time at the shallow end — managing, coordinating, responding, being present everywhere — without ever quite getting to the work that required you specifically. The role doesn't automatically give you the proportion. And it's also possible, even early in a career, to start carving out the space where your thinking matters, where something wouldn't exist without you.

So the question isn't just "what is my work made of right now?" It's "am I being intentional about how that combination evolves?"

That's a harder question, because it requires being honest about agency. Some of the proportion is genuinely outside your control — what your role demands, what the organisation needs from you at this moment, what phase you're in. But not all of it. And I think we don't ask ourselves often enough: in the parts I can shape, am I shaping them toward the work that's actually mine?

I keep coming back to this when we talk about work-life balance. The conversation often assumes we know what the work side of that equation is made of — and I'm not sure we do. Or at least, I'm not sure I always do. If I haven't reflected on the proportion within my work, and whether I'm moving toward it with any intention, then what am I balancing against the rest of my life? A feeling of busyness? A full calendar?

The first balance is inside the work itself. And it's less about judging any given day and more about developing, over time, a clearer picture of what your work actually looks like — and whether you're moving toward the version of it that's genuinely yours.

I'm still figuring out what that looks like for me. But I think the question is worth asking, and asking again, as things shift.

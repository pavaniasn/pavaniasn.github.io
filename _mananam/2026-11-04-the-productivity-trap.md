---
title: "The Productivity Trap: Why Saving Time Just Doubled Our Work"
slug: the-productivity-trap
summary: >-
  AI saved us a week. Where did the days go?
date: 2026-11-04
---

A friend once asked a very innocent question in a policy class. We were talking about productivity, about how AI helps you be 10x productive, 100x productive. She turned to me and said, "But I don't want to be 10x productive." She was okay with what she could do. That was about a year and a half ago, when all these AI tools were just babies. Her question has stayed with me ever since.

Think of a report that used to take a week. Now it takes a day. Writing the report was your job, and you finished it. So what do you do with the other six days? Where do they go?

In the same policy class, the professor spoke about prosperity. For human beings, prosperity has been about saving time. Agriculture was the first technology we had, and it helped us save time. Matt Ridley, in The Rational Optimist, puts it this way: "This is what prosperity is: the increase in the amount of goods and services you can earn with the same amount of work." His example is a Babylonian worker who had to work for hours to get one hour of lamplight. If we go with this definition, AI fits well. AI is productive.

But productivity is a rate, not an absolute quantity. There is always a numerator and a denominator: work done per unit time, per unit energy, per unit effort. Time is absolute. If prosperity is really about buying back our time, productivity only measures the rate of work done, not the time saved. We improved the numerator, and the saved hours went straight back into generating more output.

I think we are all obsessed with time, because it is the one thing we can never control. So the animal mind grabs anything that promises to buy it back, hence the craze around AI. But are we actually saving time? If yes, what are we doing with this ‘saved’ time? I only see six reports being generated instead of one, not six days saved and used for life outside work.

There was a time when Alexis Ohanian, the co-founder of Reddit, called it "hustle porn": the way young people showed off extra hours like a badge. Now, regardless of what we like or what we want, all of us are pushed to show off. I am tempted to call this a new variant of hustle porn, only that here, instead of bragging about late nights, we now show off the absurd volume of output we generate.

Goodhart's law says that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. I see it everywhere: reports, appraisal forms, publication counts. I read somewhere that somebody published more than a hundred papers in a year. That is good only if they are actually contributing to knowledge. Because we lack metrics on true value, we rely on proxies that AI is now optimised to inflate. Even AI companies charge for tokens, and by default the tools use many tokens, whether or not the output is good or the work is even complete. The pricing rewards volume, not depth.

Think of desire in poetry. There is an inherent beauty in expressing human emotions, the pleasure we feel when we read a romantic poem or see a beautiful painting or sculpture. A subtle version and a raw version elicit different kinds of emotion. You try to turn it up a notch, and very quickly it turns into something vulgar. The same thing that gave you pleasure might even cause displeasure. Music, art, colour, nature: all of it depends on balance. Without a limit, it becomes obscene. Now I think we have turned the knob too far on productivity, to the point of vulgarity, moving from want to greed but without the intrinsic desire to do more.

AI optimists say AI is as basic as electricity, so why would you need a choice when your option is already good? But with electricity, I still had the choice of appliances. Nobody forces you to use a washing machine or a robot cleaner. There is my agency there. Electricity was only a medium. It sat in the background until you plugged something in. AI is pushed straight into the work itself. Here, it feels like a direct electric shock. I know the example is extreme, but maybe this moment needs an extreme example.

I am not against AI. I do see it as a tool for prosperity, like the steam engine or electricity. AI made it possible for me to publish my own site and build my own product, something that would have otherwise required a formal course in coding. There are certain tasks I would prefer not to do with AI, and certain tasks where I am super excited to use it. But regardless of my choice, I am pushed to use it for everything. In a Digital Education Council report on AI and collaborative learning, students said, "it's not that we want to use AI, we can't not use it." So I think it has become a universal problem. The issue is not AI. It is how the system is pushing it.

That fine line is what differentiates beauty from obscenity, candour from rudeness, want from greed, and productivity that truly creates progress for society from productivity chased mindlessly. I know that there is no single line for everyone but my concern is that nobody seems to be talking about that line at all. It is nowhere to be found.

In Telugu, there is a beautiful saying: పెరుగుట విరుగుట కొరకే. Growth carries the seeds of its own breaking. In a biological growth curve, every log phase eventually hits a plateau and then declines. I assume AI is in its log phase right now, and what happens as it reaches the next stage and what it does to us and our work, we have to wait and watch.

Coming back to my friend’s question, so much has changed since she asked it. Just so you know, she is one of the super achievers I know. So when a person like her questioned the need to get to 10x, maybe her question was never about 10x or 1x. It was 10x on whose terms, and by whose choice. We have all kinds of papers and reports on AI and productivity, but I would really love to see somebody study this and let us know: is AI giving us prosperity, or is this another mad rat race on steroids?

To conclude, I hope we all find that line, for ourselves, and don't take the line that the system gives us.

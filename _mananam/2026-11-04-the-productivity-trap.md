---
title: "10x on Whose Terms?"
slug: the-productivity-trap
summary: >-
  AI promised us time. We got output porn instead.
date: 2026-11-04
---

A friend once asked a very innocent question in an economics class. We were talking about productivity, about how AI helps you be 10x productive, 100x productive. She turned to me and said, "But I don't want to be 10x productive." She was okay with what she could do. That was about a year and a half ago, when all these AI tools were just babies. So much has happened since then, but her question has stayed with me.

I keep coming back to it because of a simple example. Think of a report that used to take a week. Now it takes a day. That's when people cheer, "Hooray, you saved so much time. You are being more productive." Which is true. But is it? If you finish your job in a day, what are you doing with the other six days? Where did they go?

It is not just about reports, or even work. Throughout human civilisation, we have been obsessed with time. We learned to master temperature, read the sky and work the earth. Almost everything that once seemed beyond our control, we have come to understand, and much of it we have conquered. Except time. So anything that promises to give us time back has been hugely successful in human history, and I think that is the real reason for the craze around AI. Productivity is the surface. Underneath, it is all about the time AI promises to give us back. But are we actually getting it back? I only see six reports being generated instead of one, not six days saved and used for life outside work.

And we don't just make the six reports. We show them off. Some years ago, Alexis Ohanian, the co-founder of Reddit, called it "hustle porn": the way young people showed off extra hours like a badge. Now, regardless of what we like or what we want, all of us are pushed to show off. I am tempted to call it "output porn", a new variant of hustle porn, only that here, instead of bragging about late nights, we now show off the absurd volume of output we generate.

Volume has become the target. Goodhart's law says that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. I see it everywhere: reports, appraisal forms, publication counts. I read somewhere that somebody published more than a hundred papers in a year. That is great if those papers actually contributed to knowledge in that domain, but who is measuring that? Because we lack metrics on true value, we rely on proxies that AI is now optimised to inflate. Even AI companies charge by the token, and the pricing rewards volume, not depth. I wish we were equally obsessed with outcomes, instead of just measuring outputs.

None of this means I am against AI. I do see it as a tool for prosperity, like the steam engine or electricity. AI made it possible for me to publish my own site and build my own product, something that would have otherwise required a formal course in coding. There are certain tasks in my job that I am thrilled to delegate to AI, whereas some I prefer to do on my own. Yet I feel pushed by the system to use it for everything, just to join the "output porn" club we created with AI. In a Digital Education Council report on AI and collaborative learning, students said, "it's not that we want to use AI, we can't not use it." So I think it has become a universal problem. The issue is not AI. It is how the system is pushing it.

AI optimists argue that AI is as basic as electricity, and they promise a better life with AI, just as electricity gave us. So, they ask, how would your choice matter if all your options are already good? And good here means good for output, for productivity. Agreed, let's say they are right. But still, shouldn't it be my choice, my decision to make? With electricity, I still had the choice of appliances. Nobody forces you to use a washing machine or a robot cleaner. There is my agency there. Electricity was only a medium. It sat in the background until you plugged something in. But AI is pushed straight into our lives and work, like an electric shock. I know the example is extreme, but maybe this moment needs an extreme example.

So where is the line? Think of desire in poetry. There is an inherent beauty in expressing human emotions, the pleasure we feel when we read a romantic poem or see a beautiful painting or sculpture. A subtle version and a raw version elicit different kinds of emotion. You try to turn it up a notch, and very quickly it turns into something vulgar. The same thing that gave you pleasure might even cause displeasure. Music, art, colour, nature: all of it depends on balance. Without a limit, it becomes obscene. Now I think we have turned the knob too far on productivity, to the point of vulgarity. We have moved from want to greed, obsessed with output and with no concern for outcomes. Building apps that nobody uses, creating content that no one watches, writing articles that have no energy of their own: yes, the output porn club.

That fine line is what differentiates beauty from obscenity, candour from rudeness, want from greed, and productivity that truly creates progress for society from productivity chased mindlessly. I know that there is no single line for everyone but my concern is that nobody seems to be talking about that line at all. It is nowhere to be found.

There is a second question, separate from the line. More output is not bad in itself, just as more GDP is never bad for a nation. Back in that economics class, the professor spoke about prosperity. For human beings, prosperity has been about saving time. Agriculture was the first technology we had, and it helped us save time. Matt Ridley, in The Rational Optimist, puts it this way: "This is what prosperity is: the increase in the amount of goods and services you can earn with the same amount of work." His example is a Babylonian worker who had to work for hours to get one hour of lamplight. If we go with this definition, AI fits well. AI is productive.

But productivity is a rate, not an absolute quantity. There is always a numerator and a denominator: work done per unit time, per unit energy, per unit effort, whereas time is absolute. We improved the numerator, and the saved hours went straight back into generating more output. If prosperity is really about buying back our time, did we get that?

When we rush for more output, we are really rushing for more GDP. But a productive nation is not automatically a prosperous one. Michael Green, who leads the Social Progress Index, has shown this: rich countries like Kuwait and Saudi Arabia have only middling social progress, while Costa Rica, on a modest income, comes close to some of the G7. So if more output is what you want from AI, that is fine. Just don't mistake it for prosperity.

In Telugu, there is a beautiful saying: పెరుగుట విరుగుట కొరకే. Growth carries the seeds of its own breaking. In a biological growth curve, every log phase eventually hits a plateau and then declines. I assume AI is in its log phase right now, and what happens as it reaches the next stage and what it does to us and our work, we have to wait and watch.

Coming back to my friend's question. Just so you know, she is one of the super achievers I know. So when a person like her questioned the need to get to 10x, maybe her question was never about 10x or 1x. It was 10x on whose terms, and by whose choice. We have all kinds of papers and reports on AI and productivity, but I would really love to see somebody study this and let us know: is AI giving us prosperity, or is this another mad rat race on steroids? I hope we all find that line for ourselves, and don't take the line that the system gives us.

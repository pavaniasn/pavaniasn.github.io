---
title: "Learning to Love and Loving to Learn"
slug: learning-to-love-and-loving-to-learn
summary: >-
  Nobody falls in love the same way twice. I started wondering, on a late evening
  with music playing, whether the same is true of how we love to learn.
date: 2026-09-25
---

Remember the last time you were in love?

By the way, this is not for those who are still with their first love and never had to break
their heart and fall in love again. This one is for people like me, who learned to love again.
And again.

Think about the very first time. It had its own flavour. I cannot generalise for everybody, but
you know the one — it felt a certain way. And then, for some reason, it ends. Maybe there is a
heartbreak. Maybe it simply doesn't work out and you deal with it. You move on. And then, if
you are lucky, you fall in love again.

Now tell me it felt the same.

It doesn't. I am going by my own experience, and by all the stories I have heard from friends
and from people in my circle.

I also know people who have fallen in love many times. Let's not go to the place where we judge
them for it. Let's not dissect it and question whether it was really love — let's assume that
every single time, the emotion was sincere. Because even then, something is different. Not the
emotion. What is different is how they meet it. Every time they fall, they bring different
prior knowledge to it.

Sorry for using the learning science term. But I think it is needed for the segue into what I
want to say next.

As someone moves through the cycles, they tend to become more mature, more aware of the feeling
— less of the wildness and the unknownness, more of the known and the familiar.

And as that happens, something else slowly falls away. The external motivations that sit around
love — security, societal expectations, validation — begin to loosen their hold. We love simply
because we want to. Because loving another human being, and being able to experience that
feeling in our hearts, is itself the reward.

Though all of this comes with a condition. It assumes you went back and sat with what happened.
We all know someone who has fallen in love a couple of times and walks into the next one
exactly as they walked into the first — the same blind spots, the same mistakes, the same story
told again with a different name in it. Living through something is not the same as learning
from it, and nobody ever sits us down and explains the difference.

I was turning this over on a late evening, nothing but music playing, thinking about my own
story and my friends' stories, when the thought arrived sideways — does this apply to our love
for learning too? Not just our love for a person.

I would like to call myself a lifelong learner, and to stay true to that to the best of my
abilities. So let me trace it.

I remember learning in school. It was fun. I liked science; those were some of my favourite
subjects, and there were others I didn't enjoy much. I knew what I was learning, even if not
fully. But what kept me at it was mostly external — the board exams, admission into a good
college.

Then I got to college, picked my discipline, went deep into science, and enjoyed it more than I
had before. But there was another external motivation waiting there too: a degree, a placement,
publications.

And there was a reason for all of it. I was buried under what my family and the society around
me expected of me, and given our economic situation those expectations were not unreasonable.
Acing the exams and doing well was the whole of it. Reading outside academia was not simply
crowded out by all this — I was taught that it was a waste of time and effort. So I didn't do
it. For years.

It is not that I didn't love learning then. I did. But reflecting back, I can see the
difference between the way I loved it in school and the way I loved it in college. The same
word. Two different things.

I read well, but I read only inside my discipline — academic books, research articles. Reading was work that happened to be enjoyable. Then one day, in a staff meeting, a
former superior of mine recited a few lines about the power of the human will, from James Allen's *As a Man
Thinketh*. He was trying to inspire the room. It moved me enough that I bought the book that
same day and read it.

Until that day, my goal with reading had been to learn something — academically, or for
knowledge. That was the first time I experienced reading as joy.

Today I have a collection of books at home that has nothing to do with my work. Nothing at all.

But that did not happen overnight. Somewhere in those years, what I was after stopped being
information and knowledge; it became the act of reading itself. It took years of reflection to
get there — working out my own style, what I like, what inspires me, whose writing moves me
most. I no longer go by anybody's recommendations. I have my own taste now, and I curate my own
library. Nobody taught me how to do that either.

And what I love about them now is not only what I take away from them. It is the beauty of
human thought itself — that a person was able to build something like this in their mind, and
then put it out in language this good. It could be anything. Poetry. Science. A piece on where
technology is going. Somebody's take on geopolitics. The idea that this thought was processed
in a human mind and came out like *this* — that is sheer joy for me.

That is the kind of learning I recognise now when I hold the two side by side. There is no
degree at the end of it, no placement, no publication. I don't get anything from that
relationship — and I still experience the joy of it. Just like the reward of loving on its own,
without expecting anything in return.

Since I work in education, once I started comparing the two — how we learn to love as we age,
and how we love to learn as we age — I couldn't leave it alone.

Is it age? With years, prior knowledge accumulates, so perhaps we simply understand things
better, love and learning alike.

But I am not sure I believe that, because we all know people for whom wisdom has nothing to do
with the number of years behind them.

So is it the reflection?

If it is, then the obvious move is to put reflection into the classroom. And we already have.
We hand out reflection journals. We close the unit with three questions.

But handing someone a journal does not teach them to reflect. Learning to do it properly took
practice, and it took time, and nobody ever taught me how.

So the question I am left with is not whether reflection belongs in a classroom. It is already
there. It is whether we are teaching anyone how to do it.

I don't have the answer, but it is something that will guide me in designing my next learning
experience.

---

*P.S. — Everything above is a personal opinion, and I would ask you not to read it as a
universal claim. In education we have known for centuries that one size does not fit all. If
that is true for matters of the mind, I would not expect it to be any truer for matters of the
heart.* :)

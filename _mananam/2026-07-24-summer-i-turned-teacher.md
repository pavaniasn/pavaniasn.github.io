After my research years, I had to choose between postdoc research/industry, or teaching. Honestly, I wasn't chasing teaching. People kept telling me I was good at communicating ideas clearly, so I thought, maybe I am, let me try. And it worked. My students were happy. I was happy. No, this is not the end of story.

Somewhere in there, I started noticing the other problems around me: administration, leadership, management, the support systems faculty needed and didn't have. I'd watch colleagues who were accomplished scholars struggle with the little things, partly because no one had ever trained them for that part of the job, and partly because the system itself made it hard. Myself included. That's what pulled me out of the classroom and when I decided to make education my career, I chose to focus on building the systems that empower teachers.

The work so far has been satisfying. I don't think I ever consciously missed the classroom as there's a lot of facilitation in faculty development too, just aimed at a different room. In my five years away from teaching, there were only two moments that pulled me back to what it feels like to be a teacher, really feel it from the inside.

The first was watching my professors at Harvard: Kathy, Matt, Joe, and many more. Sitting in Matt's class, in Kathy's office hours, watching how they connected with students, how much it shaped me as a person and the way I approach education. That was the first time the power of a teacher gave me goosebumps and, right behind it, a small doubt about whether I'd been right to leave the classroom at all. I knew I wasn't Kathy or Matt. But even a fraction of who they were felt like enough to matter. It was powerful, but fleeting; a thought I didn't hold onto for long.

The second moment is this summer, when the team, especially Prof. Sudarshan, gently nudged me to handle a few sessions in our internship program — that program deserves its own article, so I'll leave it there for now.

My first reaction was anxiety. I've never thought of myself as a great teacher, and I'd be doing this alongside Sudarshan and Prakash, who come alive the moment they're in front of students. Watching the two of them with a room is a bit like watching that scene "O Captain, my Captain," from the movie, *Dead Poet's society*. I'm not that teacher so standing next to them added its own pressure.

But like most big decisions in my life, I landed on: what the hell, let me just try.

I ended up taking sessions all summer, and I still do. I don't know if I'm doing justice to my students. Nobody handed me a feedback form or a course eval, and with 600, sometimes 700, students on a single Zoom call, there's never really been a mechanism for that. What I got instead were screens filling up with hearts and smiling-face emojis throughout the session, and LinkedIn posts where students tagged me afterward, saying something had landed. That's the only evidence I've got, and somehow it's enough.

I still don't know how much of what I said will stay with them but I know what will stay with me. I know one of them draws. I know one of them photographs. I know one of them wrote a script for an entire web series, ten seasons of it! I know some of them built creative features for our open source projects I never would have thought of. I know some of them show up every single day despite challenges I can only guess at. And one of them has two pets, Tyson and Jenny, who somehow found their way into our conversations too. I also know the quiet ones; the ones who never spoke a single word in two months but their smiles and nods in a small box on my 14" screen were my cues while I was speaking, my silent cheerleaders through every session.

Yesterday, in a team conversation, when each of us were reflecting on our milestones, on what we'd each remember decades from now, I didn't say it out loud then but I know the answer: this summer. The summer I turned teacher. One of the chapters I'll bookmark in my life's journey.

I want to be clear, though. This isn't me quietly planning a return to the classroom. I'm just glad that my work doesn't force me to choose. I get to work on my mission, building programs that help faculty become better teachers at scale, while staying close enough to be part of the change that happens in a single classroom too. The way I see it, there's the first-order change a good teacher makes with their own class, and there's the second-order change that ripples out when a faculty member walks away better equipped. This summer, for a few weeks, I got to stand in both. And I don't plan to make it the last one :)

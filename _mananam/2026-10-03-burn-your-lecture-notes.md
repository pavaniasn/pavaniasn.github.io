I had heard the name Ashtavakra many times. I had never actually known the character, or the
story around him. It was just a name I was familiar with.

For those of you who are not familiar with him either, his story is a small episode in the
Mahabharata. Ashtavakra was a boy cursed by his own father for pointing out a mistake in his
recital. Years later, the same boy freed his father by winning a debate against the scholar
Vandi in King Janaka's court.

Then one evening I was watching an online video of Mahabharata stories, and there he was. My
son and I sat together trying to understand it: who this boy was, and why a king would come
to him as a student. The more we pieced it together, the more it inspired me.

One line from that video stayed with me. Knowledge is borrowed memory, and what stays back once
the borrowed memory fades is wisdom. I couldn't let go of it. The next morning I discussed it
with my team, and a couple of days later with my students in an online class.

I tried to understand why it held me so tightly. As I keep sharing and writing, I learn.

I love learning a lot. And the more I learn, the more I understand that my memory is limited.
It can't be unlimited. So how much to put in my memory is a skill I am actively working on.
Which details can I discard, because they are anyway available later when I need them? And what
is the foundational idea, the thing that can become a habit of my mind?

That has been my priority, a learning habit I picked up during my time at Harvard, in
conversations with my friends there. Learning never ends. But we don't memorise everything we
learn. What matters is knowing what to discard, what to keep, and the essence of what we keep.

Take frameworks. There are so many of them in my field. If you ask me, I might not be able to
recite every detail of one the way another professional would. But many times I have caught
myself applying those frameworks, properly, without the misconceptions, the way they are
meant to be used.

Those are moments of reassurance. Maybe I am not able to read out all the bullets. But I know
what it means, where to apply it, and how to use it in my own thinking and in programme design.
That is where I want to get to.

## Forget it all

Now, coming back to the sage Ashtavakra we spoke about in the beginning. In the Ashtavakra Gita,
King Janaka comes to him as a student and asks how anyone comes to real knowledge. This is what
Ashtavakra tells him: *"You may recite the scriptures, or listen to them, again and again. Still
you will not be settled in yourself until you have forgotten it all."*

Which seems counterintuitive.

Where it gets more interesting is this. When I heard this statement for the first time, I
immediately connected it to something I had read a couple of weeks earlier, a piece by Alfred
North Whitehead, a philosopher from Cambridge.

## The second half: a philosopher, a century ago

What does he have to say?

In the 1920s, Whitehead wrote about the rhythm of learning. It
moves, he said, through three stages. First romance, when something grabs you and you are
curious before you understand it. Then precision, the hard part, the rules and facts and
practice. Then generalisation, which he called *"the stage of shedding details in favour of the
active application of principles, the details retreating into subconscious habits."*

And then he wrote: *"In a sense, knowledge shrinks as wisdom grows: for details are swallowed up
in principles."*

Writing about what a university education should leave you with, he put it even more boldly:
*"Your learning is useless to you till you have lost your text-books, burnt your lecture notes,
and forgotten the minutiae which you learnt by heart for the examination."*

Burn your lecture notes. Forget it all. Two people, some two thousand years apart, saying the
same thing about learning.

Neither of them is saying the details don't matter. Whitehead would never let you skip
precision. You can only forget what you once truly knew.

## What this means for education

The details, the stage Whitehead calls precision, are of course needed. The exams we set test
them, and they get us to a certain point. But they are not the final destination, right? So
where in our education are we leaving room for the wisdom that the sage and Whitehead talk
about?

I see this in education research too. We have so many theories and frameworks, and most of the
time I see people drowning in all the details of them. The bullets, the stages, the
sub-components. And somewhere in that, the essence gets forgotten: what does this framework, or
this philosophy, actually mean for our students and for our educators?

Thanks to AI, the changes we are seeing in how we teach and assess, and in what we consider
learning, are positive. Things are shifting, and it is exciting to see us slowly moving towards
the generalisation stage, because AI can now handle much of the precision, right? A few
innovations are happening in pockets. To see them across all our systems will take time and a
lot of effort. I am excited to be bringing this idea into the projects I am working on now.

## Still on the way

Thinking about this and writing this piece has been an interesting episode and a time of
reflection for me. I hope I'll keep learning how to learn.

Thanks to the sage Ashtavakra and to Whitehead for reassuring me that I am on the right track,
and that I need not worry. 😊

I had heard the name Ashtavakra many times, but I didn't really know much about the character
or the story about him.

For those of you who are not familiar with him or his story either, just know that it is a
small episode in the Mahabharata. Ashtavakra is a sage. As a boy, he was cursed by his own
father for pointing out a mistake in his recital. Years later, the same boy ends up winning a
debate against the scholar Vandi in King Janaka's court, and ultimately frees his father.
That's the gist of it. There is a lot more, but even I don't know the details. You can look it
up in the Mahabharata or the Ashtavakra Gita.

One of the routines I have with my son is that we try to learn something together: about
Indian culture, our regional language, the epics, the history of India, or sometimes world
history. In that course, I bumped into an online video about the argument between Ashtavakra
and Vandi, which explained the whole argument in 13 questions. If you're wondering why I'm telling all this, I'm
just getting to the point.

What stayed with me is one line from the video: *"Knowledge is borrowed memory, and what stays
back once the borrowed memory fades is wisdom."* It was so interesting that I couldn't let go
of it.

## Checking the source

By habit, I'm a scientist. I always look things up. I wanted to find out if he actually said
that, and I found the verse in the Ashtavakra Gita (16.1) that comes closest to it. There,
Ashtavakra tells King Janaka: *"You may recite the scriptures, or listen to them, again and
again. Still you will not be settled in yourself until you have forgotten it all."*

I also found out that what the video put inside the debate is not actually in the debate. It
comes up later, in a different episode: Ashtavakra's conversation with King Janaka.

This proves to me, once again, that it's important to check the evidence when you find
something interesting, no matter what the source is: AI, Chrome, or an online YouTube video.

And no, this is not the main point of this article. I am getting there.

## A century-old book saying the same thing

The verse was interesting for me as a learner and an educator. But what was more interesting
is something I had read a couple of weeks earlier, on a completely different note. It said
the same thing, but with a different vocabulary.

I was reading *The Aims of Education* by Alfred North Whitehead, where he discusses something
called the rhythm of education. I loved it, because most of the ideas from a century-old book
still seem relevant to today's education. That was both surprising and shocking for me.

He presents the rhythm in three stages:

1. **Romance**, where you fall in love with the subject.
2. **Precision**, where you learn all the details.
3. **Generalisation**, where you shed those details and what stays becomes a mental habit. In
   his words, *"the stage of shedding details in favour of the active application of
   principles, the details retreating into subconscious habits."*

The other interesting part about these stages is that they don't apply only to a single
lesson. They apply to the education system as a whole. If you look at the whole system, you
see:

- **Primary school** as a place for romance, where the child falls in love with different
  subjects.
- **Middle and high school** as a place for precision, where they learn all the details.
- **University** as a place where they form the mental habits required to become a
  professional in that field.

He talks about the university as the place for the generalisation stage. That got me
thinking: we are focusing too much on precision everywhere, and too little on romance, right
from school.

And this is what he says about it: *"Your learning is useless to you till you have lost your
text-books, burnt your lecture notes, and forgotten the minutiae which you learnt by heart for
the examination."* And elsewhere: *"In a sense, knowledge shrinks as wisdom grows: for details
are swallowed up in principles."*

It's so similar to what the verse from the Ashtavakra Gita says. It was super interesting for
me to see two people talking about the same thing in different times and different contexts.
One is a sage (or a character) from a 2,000-year-old epic, and the other is a philosopher from a century ago. It was amazing when my brain formed that
connection, and I could feel it.

## What it means for me as a learner

Since this blog is my Mananam, my reflection, it kept me questioning: how does this apply to
me as a learner?

I love learning a lot. And the more I learn, the more I understand that my memory is limited.
I can't memorise everything. But I also know that one definition of learning is putting
something into long-term memory. Of course, this is one definition out of many, but I feel it
has its own place. So now, as I learn new things, I must also learn which of those have to be
memorised.

So how do I balance the two: knowing that my memory is limited, and knowing that a part of
learning is being able to hold things in long-term memory?

Inspired by the conversations I had with my friends during my time at Harvard, I slowly started
paying more attention to which details I can let go of, because they are anyway available later
when I need them, and what the foundational idea is, the thing that can become a habit of my
mind.

Take frameworks. There are so many of them in my field. If you ask me, I might not be able to
recite every detail of one the way another professional would. But many times I have caught
myself applying those frameworks, properly, without the misconceptions, the way they are meant
to be used.

Those are moments of reassurance. Maybe I am not able to read out all the bullets. But I know
what it means, where to apply it, and how to use it in my own thinking and in programme design.

So a framework is both things for me. It is a mental habit, where I know exactly how to think
with it and bring it up in the right context. And it is something I refer to, because I don't
have all the details in my head.

## From me to the system

The details, the stage Whitehead calls precision, are of course needed. The exams we set test
them, and they get us to a certain point. But they are not where learning ends, right? So
where in our education are we leaving room for the wisdom that the sage and Whitehead talk
about?

Thanks to AI, the changes we are seeing in how we teach and assess, and in what we consider
learning, are positive. Things are shifting, and it is exciting to see us slowly moving towards
the generalisation stage, because AI can now handle much of the precision, right? A few
innovations are happening in pockets. To see them across all our systems will take time and a
lot of effort. I am excited to be bringing this idea into the projects I am working on now.

## Still on the way

Again, as with anything, I'm not saying that this is the only definition of learning, or the
only way knowledge is created or wisdom is developed. This is one of the interesting arguments
I came across, and it is worth spending some time reflecting on. I definitely picked up
something useful, good food for thought, from both of these pieces, and I hope you do too.

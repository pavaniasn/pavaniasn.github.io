Recently, in a discussion at work, a question came up that I could not stop thinking about. Classrooms everywhere use points, badges and ranks, and we know they push individual learners to do more. But what do they do to the class as a whole?

I loved the question the moment I heard it. Contributing to it, though, seemed to need some knowledge of economics, game theory and the study of networks, and I had none. What I had was curiosity. In this piece I want to show you how I approached it, and how my curiosity tends to work.

## Two ways to explore

There are two ways to get to know a new place. You can stay in one spot and come to know it deeply. Or you can hop, the way people do on island or city tours, spending a little time at many stops, so that by the end you have a picture of the whole place that no single stop could have given you. And if a stop turns out not to matter, you still learnt something by seeing why.

With a question this new to me, I chose to hop.

## My stops

The first stop was economics, and it came with a lot of jargon: equilibrium, optimum, payoffs. A few years ago, words like these would have stopped me. I would have taken them as a sign that this was not my cup of tea and turned back. This time I did not let them intimidate me or dampen my curiosity. I broke each idea down until I could say it in simple words, and then I carried on with the question. That, for me, was a small sign of how I have grown as a learner. The one idea I carried away was that what is best for each person is not always best for the group.

The next stop was the philosophy of education, and it gave me my first aha. I already had views on whose progress should count in a classroom: the learners who are struggling, the class added together, or everyone reaching at least a basic level. I also had a sense that education is worth something in itself, not only as a way of getting ahead of others. Philosophers have argued over these for a long time, and each position has a name, which I did not know before reading up for this question. It felt like some of my days at Harvard, when I kept thinking, "I knew this all along as a teacher and a practitioner. Now I know what it's called."

Psychology came next, pulled from the memory of a session I took for students back in the summer. Leon Festinger's idea, from the 1950s, is that we judge ourselves by comparing ourselves with others, mostly with people who are similar to us. It matched something I had felt: when someone is far ahead of you, you stop comparing and just do your own work. Rewards make comparison visible, and comparison can push someone forward or quietly pull them back.

Then came a stop that, on the surface, had nothing to do with points and badges. It came from some random reading last winter. In 1998 Neil Postman gave a talk on five things we need to know about technological change. My brain simply swapped "technology" for "reward system", and every one of them still made sense:

- Every technology is a trade-off. A reward system gives something and takes something away.
- "There are always winners and losers, and the winners always try to persuade the losers that they are really winners." This needed no translation at all.
- Every technology carries an idea about the world. A reward system carries an idea of what is valued.
- Change is ecological. A new technology changes the whole environment rather than adding one thing to it, and a reward system changes the whole class, not only the people who win.
- Technology tends to become a myth, something we stop questioning. We rarely ask why there has to be a topper.

From there I went to the idea of a hidden curriculum, which Ivan Illich wrote about in *Deschooling Society* (1971): schools teach through how they are run as much as through what is on the syllabus. I see this in many schools around where I live, including my child's. They say they value every child, that every child is cared for and every child's progress matters. Then they have a student of the year and a wall of badges for the top few. I am not saying either one is good or bad. My question is whether they are even aware that they are doing two different things, and that some of their actions work against the culture they want to build. I see this in many educational institutions, and noticing it was another aha.

The biggest aha was how these stops came together. Sometimes, when you are looking too deep, it helps to lift your head and look around. Suddenly you have the answer, or in my case, a better-stated question. Not the answer, but for me that is just as good.

## Where the hopping left me

I started with "do rewards work?" I ended with a question about everyone in the room, not just the people who win: who goes up, who goes down, who does not move, and what the class as a whole becomes.

## Keeping the ball moving

A really good friend and I have a phrase for one of our favourite things. We call it idea ping pong. You throw out a half-formed thought, it comes back sharper, and you go back and forth until something takes shape. It is difficult to find people on that frequency, and even when you do, they are not always around. Yes, that's life!

For this question, I used AI to keep the ball moving. This is how I usually work with it. I keep asking questions, and with each response I go back and forth, refining the question each time. AI is only there to help me widen the map, and I make sure I am the one steering. In unfamiliar territory it is tempting to hand over the wheel to whoever seems to know the way, and AI can seem to know everything because of how much it has been trained on. I don't think that works. In this case I had to keep the steering really close, and that is how I reached clarity. There were many moments where the conversation could have led to a distraction, a complete misreading or a U-turn on the idea, and I avoided them because I was holding the wheel.

## My two cents

This isn't a theory or a framework, just what worked for me this time. Take whatever is useful.

- Let the question be bigger than what you know.
- Put the jargon into plain words before you move on.
- Take one idea from each stop.
- Try the swap: change one word in an idea from another field and see if it still fits.
- Look for names for what you already know.
- Hold the wheel, especially with AI.

And one question to take back to your own classroom or workplace: does what you reward match what you say you value?

## A reward I'm not looking for

The whole process reminded me of David Epstein's *Range*, which opens with Roger Federer and Tiger Woods. Tiger specialised early; Roger played many sports before settling on tennis. Maybe, this time, my breadth turned out to be an advantage.

Or maybe I am celebrating too early, and the real test is a later post on how the problem was worked out, or a paper. But of the positions in education I came across along the way, the one I would take is that education is valuable in itself. And since this whole question is about rewards, I do not want to tie this thinking to a paper or a publication as its reward. The process was valuable to me regardless of what gets published.

Do you dive deep or hop when a new question catches you? Tell me how your curiosity works.

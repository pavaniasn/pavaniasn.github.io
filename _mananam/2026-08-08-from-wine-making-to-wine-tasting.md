There are two ways to master wine. One is to make it: years in the vineyard, one harvest a year to learn from, with everything riding on decisions you make months before you find out whether they were right. The other is to taste it: one sip, and you can tell the region, the season, the mistake. Both are real expertise. They are not the same expertise. And I've been thinking about which one the age of AI is actually asking us for.

The argument doing the rounds right now is that taste and judgment are the new differentiators, and I find it hard to disagree. When everyone has access to the same tool, the tool stops being what separates people. What separates them is what happens next: can you look at the output and know whether it's good? Can you tell what's off? Can you push the tool towards the thing you can sense but haven't yet said out loud? That sounds like a claim about the future of work. For me, it has become a very specific question about my own week.

I use AI extensively, every day, across most of what I do—drafting, structuring, thinking out loud, the first version of almost everything. And somewhere along the way, without deciding to, I stopped being the one making and became the one judging. I moved from the vineyard to the tasting room without noticing.

While doing a literature review for one of the projects I'm working on, I came across something that said you also learn when you're the one reviewing. That stayed with me. Am I actually learning anything when I'm only reviewing? Common sense says yes, of course we are. And I can feel it happening—when I read three versions of something and know immediately that the second one is right, something in me has been trained by all the times I got that call wrong. But is it the same as writing? Should it be the same? I don't know.

Knowing that I'm learning doesn't tell me what I'm learning. And that's the part I keep turning over: this is clearly a different kind of learning from the one I got by doing the work myself, so what is each kind actually good for? Where does one serve me better than the other? I don't think the answer is that one is superior. They build different capacities. Making builds the ability to produce. Judging builds the ability to recognise.

My own very unscientific, common-sense way of understanding this comes from the movies. I can walk out of a film and say, with total confidence, that it was bad. But I cannot make a good movie. My ability to judge arrived long before any ability to produce. And notice what my verdict is missing: I can tell you the film didn't work, but I can't tell you what it would have taken to make it work. My verdict as an audience is real, but it is thin because I have never made a movie.

That is where I think the two kinds of learning separate. Reviewing shows me the range: when I look at ten versions of something, I see the space of what is possible, and that is not something producing one version can teach me. But producing teaches something reviewing can't—what it costs to get from nothing to something, which parts are genuinely hard, and how it feels to be stuck at eleven at night with no idea how to get out. That knowledge is what makes my judgment of somebody else's work fair rather than merely confident. So there are tasks where I'd now happily learn by reviewing, and tasks where I don't think anything substitutes for having made the thing. Working out which is which strikes me as the interesting question, and I haven't found anyone doing it carefully.

Here is where this stops being a personal reflection, because I don't only learn. I design learning systems for others: both students and faculty. And our education systems are built almost entirely for the vineyard. We teach the making. We assess the making. A learner's worth is what they produced, from scratch, alone. Meanwhile, a great deal of the work they are walking into will ask them to judge, direct, reject, and refine what a machine produced first—and we have very little pedagogy for that, almost no assessment for it, and barely a shared vocabulary. Which raises the question I find genuinely hard: does a good taster need to have made wine?

The easy answers—"you need the fundamentals," or "taste is taste"—are both too clean. What I suspect is that the making matters for one reason: it lets you say why. A taster who has never been near a fermentation tank can say, "This is bad." A taster who has can say, "This is bad because the grapes were picked late." Only the second sentence tells you what to do next, and that is exactly what my day looks like. "This isn't working" is not an instruction a tool can use; "this is too abstract in the second half because it stopped using examples" is. So some amount of vineyard is non-negotiable. How much, I don't know, and I doubt it's the same in every field. "Enough to know why" is not something I can turn into a syllabus.

Underneath all of it sits the question I can't get past: if taste is what matters, how do we actually teach it? A simple internet search for how to become a good wine taster will give you the five S's and "keep practising." True, and completely useless—in the same way "write every day" is true and useless. Any serious taster will tell you it's more than the technique and more than the repetition. But when I push on what the more is, I run out of road.

I want to be careful here, because the clean version of this story would be that I left the vineyard and moved into the tasting room, and that isn't what's happening. Nobody moves entirely. What I notice is a slow drift. A little more judgment, a little less production. And increasingly, a third thing that isn't quite either—production through recognition rather than production from scratch. I still make things. I just make them by choosing, rejecting, redirecting, and shaping, instead of by starting with a blank page. Whether that counts as making, and what it is doing to me over the years, I genuinely cannot tell you.

These questions come back to me in circles. I think I have settled one, and a week later it returns in a slightly different form. And it matters more than it would if this were only about me, because I am making design decisions for other people's learning on questions I haven't resolved for my own. So this isn't a piece with an answer. It's an invitation to compare notes. If you're somewhere on this same drift—a bit less making, a bit more judging—where exactly are you? What have you noticed changing? And have you found anything that actually builds taste, other than time?

P.S. In case it isn't obvious: this piece had both parts in it. Some of it I made, some of it I tasted and sent back, and it went around in circles a few times before it was done. Which is, I suppose, the only honest demonstration I have.
